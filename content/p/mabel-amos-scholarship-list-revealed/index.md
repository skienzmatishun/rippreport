[ROB HOLBERT](https://www.lagniappemobile.com/users/profile/rholbert/) - [Lagniappe](https://www.lagniappemobile.com)

The special fiduciary’s report in the long-running civil suit involving the Mabel Amos Memorial Fund not only states that board members indulged in self-dealing in handing out scholarships to relatives and friends, but the list of recipients that had been hidden from public view for the past 12 years is a virtual “who’s who” of Andalusia’s most prominent citizens.

Lagniappe has obtained a copy of the report, which has not been publicly released by Montgomery County Circuit Court Judge Greg Griffin. Compiled by Special Fiduciary James C. White, Sr., a CPA, the report had been a bone of contention in the now-4-year-old lawsuit, with Alabama Attorney General Steve Marshall twice attempting to get the state’s Supreme Court to derail efforts by Griffin to have an independent accounting of the fund conducted, first by a special master, then by a special fiduciary. The second of those attempts was rejected by the Supreme Court.

Ethics Commission unaware of its director’s controversy

At the center of the report sits Alabama Ethics Commission Executive Director Tom Albritton, whose law firm drew up the formation of the charitable trust in Mabel Amos’ last will and testament when she was in her 90s. Albritton and his law partner Rick Clifton were installed by the will’s language giving the Albritton firm two of three board member positions overseeing the Mabel Amos Memorial Fund, and John Bell of Regions Bank served as the third member, a representative of the bank acting as trustee.

Clifton died in June of 2025, Bell resigned from the board in 2021 and was replaced as the Regions representative by Drew McNeese, who also has left the board. The court removed Albritton’s involvement in selecting scholarships in 2023, and he formally resigned from the board in March of this year.

Amos, a two-term Alabama Secretary of State, left roughly $500,000 upon her death for the creation of the fund, stipulating that it was to provide scholarships for Alabama students based upon four criteria: “The character of the individual; the intelligence of the individual, which shall be above average; the scholastic record of the individual; and the financial need of the individual.”

White’s report is particularly critical of disbursements that went to children of people closely connected to the Albritton Law, including Albritton himself, whose son and daughter received a combined total of $135,000 in scholarships, according to the report. Those distributions to his children began in 2012 — three years before he was chosen to lead the Alabama Ethics Commission — but continued until 2019, four years after he took his position as the state’s ethics enforcer.

Albritton told Lagniappe in 2021 when this story first broke that he had abstained from voting for money for his children, instead leaving it to his law partner Clifton and Bell to vote on the awards. He denied self-dealing or engaging in anything else unethical.

Judge asked to sanction state ethics chief

“Regarding my kids’ scholarships, there’s no restriction within the trust for who can get it. Where my kids are concerned, I didn’t participate in that decision. It was made by one of the other trustees and a Regions representative, who at the time was John Bell,” Albritton explained in 2021.

White’s report, however, claims self-dealing is exactly what took place, citing violations of three articles of the fund’s formation.

“Disbursements on behalf of students appear to violate Article V, part E and F as it relates to personal inurement and self-dealing. Particularly, when scholarships are awarded to the children of the members of the Board and associates of the members of the Board. There were several scholarships awarded by the Trust that fit into that category and totaled $257,844,” White wrote.

Specifically, White pointed out six recipients who had direct connections to Albritton and Clifton and what they received.

1. Martha H. Albritton University of Texas at Austin — $67,500

2. James Albritton University of Texas at Austin — $67,500

Both are Albritton’s children.

3. Anna E. Bowden Southern Methodist University — $60,000

Anna Bowden is the daughter of Judge Ben Bowden who was a partner

in the law firm of Albritton, Clifton, Alverson, Moody and Bowden, P.C. and now sits on the Alabama Court of Appeals. In a 2021 interview, Bowden admitted his daughter received $45,000 in. scholarships when asked. He and Albritton are also childhood friends.

4. Chandlar Dauphin Auburn University/ University of Alabama — $26,120. Dauphin is the granddaughter of Paula Tillman who was Rick Clifton’s secretary at the law firm of Albritton, Clifton, Alverson, Moody and Bowden, P.C., according to the report.

5. Megan Ballard Lurleen B Wallace/Troy University — $14,997

6. Whitney Ballard Lurleen B. Wallace/Troy University — $21,727

The Ballards are the children of Tina Ballard who was a secretary at the law firm of Albritton, Clifton, Alverson, Moody & Bowden, P.C., according to the report.

White also pointed to distributions made directly to schools as violations of the trust’s bylaws. Nearly $470,000 was handed out to colleges and universities as “endowed scholarships,” White wrote, stating that doing so was not in concert with the articles forming the Mabel Amos Memorial Fund.

Altogether, White tallied $725,000 in unallowed spending that would be required to “make this Trust whole.”

Marshall’s representatives in the case have been trying for two years to push a settlement deal negotiated with Regions bank that they’ve said would make the fund “whole,” but they have never made the terms of that agreement public or shared it with counsel for the other plaintiffs in the case. One of the main reasons Griffin stated for wanting a full accounting of the expenditures is that he would have no way of knowing if a settlement was fair or not without it.

‘The list’

When Mabel Amos died in 1999 at the age of 99, it triggered the formation of her eponymous fund. According to IRS 990 records, Albritton, Clifton and Bell handed out rather meager scholarships during the first 10 years of its existence, although White wrote in his report that, “It must be noted here that the scholarship application process was practically nonexistent earlier in the life of the Trust.”

From 2002 through 2013, those 990 forms carried the names of recipients and scholarship amounts. However, by 2014, the discovery of oil on Amos-owned land in Conecuh County had poured millions of dollars into the fund, scholarships grew much larger and the names of recipients were no longer listed in those public reports.

Lawyers bail on Mabel Amos suit

White’s list of recipients who met the criteria for self-dealing were largely already known prior to his report being written, either via Lagniappe’s reporting or from plaintiff’s attorney Byron B. Mathews, Jr.’s filings in the case. Who received the rest of the nearly $4 million handed out by Albritton, Clifton and Bell was unclear until now.

White’s report lists the names of all recipients between 2014 and 2024, and the amount of scholarship money they were awarded during that time. Lagniappe has been able to identify the parents of many of those on the list, and they include many prominent Covington County citizens.

For example, several families with administrative positions in the PowerSouth Energy Cooperative received scholarships. The Albritton Law Firm represented The Alabama Electrical Cooperative — PowerSouth’s predecessor — in the 1960s and 1980s in different lawsuits. Albritton did not respond to questions about whether his firm continued to represent PowerSouth during the time scholarships were disbursed.

Other names on the list appear to be tied to prominent Andalusia city employees, local attorneys, a school superintendent and a principal, a former editor of the local newspaper, bankers, doctors, heads of large businesses and even a state representative. Lagniappe has reached out to some of the parents of scholarship recipients to confirm their children were awarded scholarships from the Mabel Amos fund and to discover how application was made for the scholarships. None have responded prior to publication of this story.

In answering interrogatories from the plaintiffs that were filed with the court on May 26 of this year, Albritton admitted to some of the accusations levied against him since the beginning of the suit. For example, in the 2012 990, there is a $15,000 scholarship listed as having been awarded to “Martha A. Hunter.” Plaintiffs have long alleged that was actually an award to Albritton’s daughter, Martha Hunter Albritton that was not identified as going to a board member.

The 2013 990 Form shows a $15,000 scholarship was awarded to Martha H. Albritton and accompanied by a notation identifying her as “Daughter of Board Member.” Those notations were present on all of the awards to Albritton’s children, even from 2014 - 2019 when the 990s no longer listed recipients’ names.

In his response to interrogatories, Albritton admitted the 2012 distribution indeed went to his daughter, but denied the transposed name was an attempt to “disguise the true identity of the scholarship recipient,” as plaintiffs content. Albritton claimed to have had nothing to do with it, stating that, “All disclosures were handled solely by Regions Bank.”

Questioned about the $60,000 in scholarships awarded to Bowden’s daughter from 2014 - 2017, Albritton denied he was responsible for his boyhood friend and former law partner’s child receiving one of the largest payouts in the fund’s history, claiming the board composed of himself, Clifton and Bell made that decision.

“All scholarship awards were made by the agreement of all board members, including Regions Bank,” Albritton answered, which would seem to conflict with his 2021 statement to Lagniappe that he did not participate in the awarding of scholarships to his children.

“Don’t forget that at the time the decision was made about my kids, I was still in private practice. At that time, however, I just didn’t see the point in creating an issue where there wasn’t one by putting myself out there with a vote,” he explained. “I still tell people in similar circumstances, if they don’t have to vote then don’t because you can never control what someone’s inference will be from your vote, and most people are not going to take the time to investigate whether it’s allowed or not [as it is here]. They’ll just presume you can’t vote on matters like that, so I didn’t.”

The time frame of those scholarship awards to Bowden’s daughter is also of note, because Bowden served as Covington County Probate Court judge from 2008 - 2016. Albritton admitted in his answer to the plaintiffs’ questions that members of his firm appeared before Bowden during that time.

Albritton denied knowledge of scholarships that went to the children and grandchildren of secretaries at his law firm, claiming he was “without sufficient information to admit or deny” plaintiffs’ questions about them.

The questions posed by plaintiffs also raised the issue of scholarships going to the children of Albritton firm clients. Asked specifically about scholarships going to the children of Dr. Charles R. Kerr and also the children of Drs. Allen and Kimberly Ward, identified by plaintiffs as clients of Albritton’s firm, the ethics chief admitted they did receive money. However, when pressed as to whether firm clients who did not need financial assistance were given money, Albritton claimed Regions Bank determined need.

Push and pull

The fight to control the MAMF over the past five years has been storied. After Lagniappe first reported the scholarship payments to Albritton’s children in 2021, Amos’ grandniece, Leigh Manning, and great-grandniece, Megan Carmack, filed a civil suit that included Attorney General Steve Marshall as a defendant. Marshall later petitioned the court to be added to the suit as a plaintiff, citing his office’s duty to oversee charitable trusts.

In the process of becoming a plaintiff, Marshall’s office filed motions accusing Albritton of self-dealing and fraud, both of which can carry criminal penalties, but the AG never initiated a criminal investigation.

A separate suit was filed by Conecuh County student Tyra Lindsay, who was represented by attorney Byron B. Mathews, Jr. That suit was eventually consolidated with Carmack and Manning’s suit, and Mathews has served as the principal advocate for a full accounting of the monies spent, as well as the removal of Albritton, Clifton and Regions from the board overseeing the fund.

Marshall has sought to have the other plaintiffs removed from the suit, claiming only he has “standing” in the case, but has been unable to sway the court to do so. As mentioned previously, his office twice filed emergency stays with the Alabama Supreme Court to stop Griffin from appointing White as first a special master, then a special fiduciary. While the Supreme Court rejected Marshall’s second attempt, his complaint that such an appointment would prove costly to the fund appears to have proven true.

White was granted a payment of $336,725 in July 2025 by Griffin, and since then has submitted invoices for $11,000 to cover a surety bond, $108,000 for reviewing emails and scholarship payments, $52,305 for reviewing scholarship applications and payments, and $49,075 for reviewing emails regarding payments to colleges and universities, as well as for scholarships. All together, White has billed the MAMF $557,105 for his investigation of the fund’s finances, according to filed invoices and court records.

White also absolved Regions from plaintiffs’ claims that the bank had dramatically overcharged the fund for its oversight. In the 990 Forms, regions continually listed time spent overseeing the fund’s assets as five hours a week. That time estimate did not change as millions of dollars poured into the MAMF from oil royalties, prompting plaintiffs to assert that the bank’s greatly increased charges to the fund were unwarranted. White, however, did not agree with that contention.

“My review of the fees charged by Regions Bank to this Trust revealed that they were not unreasonable under the circumstances. The amount charged for managing the investments in marketable securities was entirely reasonable and in line with their published fee schedules which are comparable to those in the industry of asset management,” he wrote in his report. “The fees charged for managing the real property in connection with oil and gas leases were reasonable as well. There was also the additional function of administering the scholarship program required by the Trust. Given these duties, this made the fees charged more reasonable. That function is time consuming and unpredictable. The communication required to administer the scholarship element, dealing with high school seniors and college students, is voluminous. There is certainly the need for a full-time person to provide the services, in addition to the cost and maintenance of the appropriate software, to effectively manage this function.”

How the suit will ultimately be settled remains to be seen. A hearing was scheduled on September 3, Lagniappe has learned. Some involved in the case have said the main issues still pending are figuring out how much it will take to make the fund whole and who will pay.
